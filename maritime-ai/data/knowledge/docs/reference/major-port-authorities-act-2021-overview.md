# Major Port Authorities Act, 2021 — overview

> Reference summary compiled from public sources for the Sagar Drishti demo
> library. This is a plain-language overview in the library's own words, not
> the text of the Act; consult the official gazette text for legal use.

## What the Act is

The Major Port Authorities Act, 2021 replaced the Major Port Trusts Act, 1963
as the governing statute for India's major ports — the ports administered by
the Union government (for example Deendayal Port at Kandla, Jawaharlal Nehru
Port, Mumbai, Chennai, Visakhapatnam, Paradip, Cochin and the other centrally
administered ports). Its stated purpose is to give major ports more autonomy
and professional, board-style governance.

## Key features in plain language

- Board of Major Port Authority: each major port is run by a compact statutory
  Board (chairperson, deputy chairperson, government and stakeholder members,
  including a labour representative) in place of the older, larger port trust.
- Greater autonomy: the Board can make its own master plan for the port area,
  enter contracts, create posts, and raise loans within limits — decisions that
  previously needed central approval in many cases.
- Tariff liberalisation: Boards (and PPP concessionaires under their
  concessions) frame their own scale of rates; the former role of the Tariff
  Authority for Major Ports (TAMP) as tariff-setter was ended, with TAMP-era
  functions folded into the new scheme.
- Adjudicatory Board: an Adjudicatory Board handles specified disputes —
  notably between ports and PPP concessionaires — and residual TAMP work.
- Public-private partnership: the Act explicitly contemplates concession
  agreements for berths and terminals, which is how most modern container
  capacity at major ports is operated.
- CSR and development: Boards may spend on corporate social responsibility and
  infrastructure connected with the port.
- Penalties: a simplified penalty schedule replaces the older Act's detailed
  offence provisions.

## What it does NOT cover — and why that matters for Mundra

The Act applies only to the notified major ports. Mundra is a non-major port
in Gujarat: it is regulated under the Indian Ports Act, 1908 read with Gujarat
state law and the Gujarat Maritime Board's oversight, and is operated by a
private developer. So in the Sagar Drishti demo world, the Major Port
Authorities Act is background law — useful for benchmarking against major-port
practice (tariff structures, concession models, port authority boards) — while
the port's own conservancy powers flow from the 1908 Act framework.
